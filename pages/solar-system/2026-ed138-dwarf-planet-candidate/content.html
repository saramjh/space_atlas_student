2026 ED138: could it be the sixth dwarf planet?

Not yet. On 27 October 2026, the NSF–DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory announced the discovery of 2026 ED138, a large object beyond Neptune. It is a dwarf-planet candidate, not an officially recognized sixth dwarf planet. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) still recognizes five: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris.

The key unanswered question is whether ED138 is large enough for its own gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape. Brightness alone cannot establish its diameter without knowing how reflective its surface is. Rubin's discovery announcement ↗ · IAU classification ↗

01 · The evidence

A moving point of light, not a surface photograph.

Rubin tracked the object's movement against a crowded field of Milky Way stars. We have repeated observations of its position and brightness, not a close-up picture of its surface.

03 · Classification

Why isn't ED138 officially the sixth dwarf planet?

A promising size estimate is not the same thing as a demonstrated round shape or a formal classification.

01 · Around the SunYes — observed orbit

Rubin plus archival observations traced a trans-Neptunian orbit. The 2026 study reports a semimajor axis of 43.09 AU, eccentricity 0.139 and inclination approximately 24.4°.

02 · Gravity makes it roundNot yet confirmed

The unknown albedo leaves a wide diameter interval. Follow-up thermal or occultation measurements can constrain size; shape needs adequate evidence.

03 · Not dominant in its orbitOuter Solar System candidate

The object occupies a trans-Neptunian population. Its current status is a candidate, and this page does not independently assign an IAU classification.

04 · Not a moonYes — orbits the Sun

ED138 is observed orbiting the Sun, rather than orbiting a planet as a satellite.

The current official list remains five: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris. "Sixth dwarf planet discovered" is therefore premature. This statement is current as of 31 October 2026. Compare the IAU criteria in our Pluto and dwarf planets lesson.

04 · Follow the discovery

How was ED138 found in a crowded sky?

Its motion was linked across many images. Earlier archival detections extended its observed history rather than requiring someone to photograph a brand-new planet.

  1. 2016 onward

    Archival DECam images later extend the observational arc back by more than ten years.

  2. March–July 2026

    Rubin images record repeated detections against the dense background of Milky Way stars.

  3. 13 Oct 2026

    Matthew Holman identifies the unusual moving object while analyzing earlier observations.

  4. 27 Oct 2026

    The Minor Planet Center announces the provisional designation 2026 ED138; Rubin publishes its candidate discovery.

  5. 30 Oct 2026

    MIT reports a separate TESS precovery study using stacked 2019–2026 observations. Those earlier detections help confirm the orbit, not its shape.

Sources: Rubin discovery release; Holman et al. preliminary research; MIT TESS follow-up.

05 · Answers to common questions

The short answers.

Did astronomers discover a sixth dwarf planet in 2026?

No official sixth dwarf planet has been recognized. ED138 was announced on 27 October 2026 as a dwarf-planet candidate. The IAU's recognized list still has five.

How big is 2026 ED138 compared with Pluto?

Its estimated diameter is 612–1,530 km under the study's reflectivity assumptions, versus Pluto's approximately 2,377 km. ED138 is smaller under that reported range; its actual diameter is not yet measured precisely.

How far away is 2026 ED138?

Its orbit has a 43.09 AU semimajor axis; it was roughly 46 AU from the Sun at announcement. A semimajor axis is not an object's instantaneous distance. See what one AU means.

What would confirm its dwarf-planet status?

Further observations constraining size, surface reflectivity and roundness, together with appropriate scientific classification. Current data suggest it could qualify, not that it definitely does.

Did ED138 come from the Haumea family?

The discovery paper places ED138 near Neptune's 12:7 resonance, like Haumea. But its redder color argues against membership in Haumea's collisional family. Its exact resonance membership is still uncertain.
