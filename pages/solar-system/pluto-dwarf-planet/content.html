Why isn't Pluto a planet anymore?

Pluto didn't move, shrink, or change in any way in 2006 — the definition of "planet" changed around it. Here's the exact three-part rule, and why Pluto fails just one of the three.

Apply the IAU's rule to different objects

Choose an object and follow the three conditions. The first two conditions don't separate Pluto from Earth; the third one does.

  1. ✓
    Orbits the SunThe object follows an orbit around our star.
  2. ✓
    Nearly roundGravity is strong enough to shape it into near hydrostatic equilibrium.
  3. ✗
    Dominates its orbital neighborhoodPluto shares its orbital region with many other Kuiper Belt objects.

Pluto: dwarf planet. It meets the first two conditions but has not cleared its orbital neighborhood.

“Cleared” does not mean every small object has vanished from a planet's orbit. It describes an object's gravitational dominance over comparable bodies in that orbital region. This is the IAU's official 2006 definition for the Solar System; some planetary scientists disagree with this classification criterion.

Sources: IAU Resolution 5A (2006) and NASA/JPL explanation.

"Cleared its neighborhood" is a criterion about gravitational dominance, not a perfectly empty orbit. Pluto shares the Kuiper Belt region with many icy objects of broadly comparable scale, unlike a dominant planet.

What's simplified

It's not really about size.

01

Pluto isn't small for its neighborhood

Pluto is still the largest known dwarf planet by some measures. The issue is what surrounds it (thousands of Kuiper Belt objects), not Pluto's own size.

02

The rule is genuinely debated

Some planetary scientists argue "clearing the neighborhood" is a poor criterion since even Earth and Jupiter share their orbits with some asteroids. This page states the current official (IAU) definition, not the only possible one.
