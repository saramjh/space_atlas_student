Science behind the jump

Gravity changes the *entire* jump arc.

With the same idealized launch speed, the upward velocity falls steadily under each world's constant downward acceleration.

A reproducible comparison

Our test person has a fixed 70 kg mass. A training launcher gives the same 3.0 m/s takeoff speed everywhere, regardless of how muscles, spacesuits or fatigue might really behave. No air resistance, atmospheric drag or changing gravity with height is included.

Maximum height: v₀² / (2g). Air time: 2v₀ / g. Weight force: mass × g, in newtons. Mass stays 70 kg; force changes.

For example, an Earth jump rises about 0.46 m, while the identical Moon launch rises about 2.81 m using the rounded NASA gravity figures below. This is a numerical model, not a documented human jump record.

What is real, and what is a model?

Numerical source: NASA NSSDC Planetary Fact Sheet — rounded gravity values in m/s². NASA's giant-planet figures refer to atmospheric levels; they are not solid floors.

Visual references: Moon: NASA Apollo 17 Taurus–Littrow scene. Mars: NASA/JPL-Caltech Perseverance Jezero imagery. Earth and giant-planet imagery come from existing credited Space Atlas NASA/JPL assets. Backgrounds are illustrative context, not exact surface or atmospheric simulations.

Limitations: A fictional test deck holds the person safely on every world, including Venus and the four giants. Movement speed is an input convention and is held constant; the simulation primarily models vertical free fall. It does not simulate pressure, temperature, life support, real walking biomechanics, orbital motion or observed terrain dimensions.
