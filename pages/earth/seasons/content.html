Teacher quick use

Test the “distance causes seasons” idea.

A short classroom prompt using the same model students just explored.

Learning objective
Explain how axial tilt changes sunlight angle and day length, and distinguish that from Earth–Sun distance.
Activity
Ask students to predict June versus December, then switch the model to 0° tilt and explain what changed.
Misconception to test
“Earth is warmer in summer because it is closer to the Sun.”
Teacher experiment: test a no-tilt Earth directly 12–15 min · print or project

A controlled counterfactual: hold the orbit and light source fixed while changing only Earth's axial tilt. Ask students to predict an outcome before showing the answer.

  1. Prediction. Would northern and southern locations still have opposite summer/winter sunlight patterns if Earth were upright (0° tilt)? Write a claim and reason.
  2. Evidence · actual tilt. In the model, select Earth tilt: 23.5°, then compare June solstice and December solstice. Observe which hemisphere leans toward the Sun and how the day-length and sunlight-angle pattern changes.
  3. Evidence · zero tilt. Keep the same June and December positions. Press Compare: 0° tilt and describe the change in hemisphere contrast; the model does not measure actual temperature or weather.
  4. Reasoning. Which variable caused opposite seasonal patterns? Explain why Earth's small distance change cannot cause that contrast.

Device-free alternative: Put a fixed cool LED lamp (Sun) at the center of a classroom orbit and move a marked foam globe (Earth) to two opposite positions. Keep the axis tilted ~23.5° and pointing the same direction in the room at both positions. Repeat with the globe upright. Compare light at northern and southern midlatitudes. The teacher controls the lamp; never look directly at a bright light or use a hot bulb. Lamp distance, globe size and orbit are not to scale.

Model limit: With 0° tilt, the strong opposing tilt-driven seasonal effect largely disappears, but Earth's slightly elliptical real orbit still changes incoming sunlight for both hemispheres together. This does not predict constant weather.

Season markers and Earth–Sun distance.

Dates vary slightly from year to year. The key comparison is that Northern Hemisphere summer occurs near Earth's farthest point from the Sun, while Northern winter occurs near its closest point.

Season questions

Why tilt matters more than distance.

Q1

Does Earth's distance from the Sun cause the seasons?

No — not the familiar opposite seasons of the two hemispheres. Earth's slightly elliptical orbit does change incoming solar energy, but it affects the whole planet at once. Axial tilt changes both sunlight angle and day length in opposite ways between the hemispheres, which is the dominant seasonal effect.

Q2

Why does a 23.4° tilt matter so much?

When a hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, sunlight arrives more directly and the Sun stays above the horizon longer each day. When it tilts away, the same solar energy is spread over a larger surface area and daylight is shorter.

Q3

What if Earth had no axial tilt?

The strong tilt-driven seasonal cycle would largely disappear. Earth's eccentric orbit would still produce a smaller global annual change in incoming solar energy, so real temperatures and weather would not become perfectly constant.

What's simplified

This is a teaching model, not an ephemeris.

01

Orbit shape

The display treats Earth's orbit as essentially circular so the tilt geometry stays readable. Earth's real orbit is a slight ellipse, which is why perihelion and aphelion exist.

02

Visual emphasis

The screen layout emphasizes the direction of Earth's axis and the Sun–Earth geometry. Do not read the on-screen distances, sizes, or tilt drawing as a scale diagram.
