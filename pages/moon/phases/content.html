Read the scientific explanation (works without JavaScript)

At First Quarter, approximately half of the disk we see from Earth is illuminated. The word “quarter” refers to the Moon being about one quarter through its roughly 29.5-day phase cycle. The Sun lights half of the Moon’s entire sphere at all ordinary phases; our viewing angle changes. Ordinary phases are not Earth’s shadow. The diagram’s right-hand bright side is a standard northern-view schematic; its orientation in the real sky varies by observing location and viewing angle.

Three experiments that explain Moon phases.

Change one thing at a time and use the Earth view to test your prediction.

01

New → full Moon

Jump to New Moon, then Full Moon. The Moon itself stays half sunlit; only the viewing geometry changes.

02

Quarter does not mean 25% lit

At first and last quarter, roughly half of the visible disk is illuminated. "Quarter" describes the Moon's position in the cycle.

03

Separate phases from eclipses

Ordinary phases happen every month. Earth's shadow only causes a lunar eclipse when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up closely enough.

Teacher-ready / device-free

One Moon, three evidence checks.

A suggested Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) activity for U.S. upper elementary classrooms. No student device, login, or website is required.

Grades & time
Grades 3–5 · 30–35 minutes (suggested; not classroom-tested).
Learning objectives
Predict the bright portion of a First Quarter Moon, collect evidence from a physical or paper model, and explain why Earth’s shadow does not cause ordinary monthly phases.
Materials
Paper, pencils, three teacher-drawn Moon disks of equal size, and optionally a small matte white ball and cool LED lamp controlled by the teacher. The activity also works with only the printable diagram and drawings.
Sequence
5 minutes to predict → 12–15 minutes to observe or model → 10 minutes to write claim, evidence and reasoning → 5 minutes for a final check.

Set up the lamp, ball and observer

  1. Keep a steady, cool LED lamp well to the left of the seated observer (Earth). A teacher positions and controls it; never shine light into eyes.
  2. Hold the white ball (Moon) about an arm’s length in front of the observer, near eye height. Keep it outside the observer’s own shadow. Put the lamp several times farther away than the ball if space allows.
  3. Move the ball to one side of the line from observer to lamp: from above, the observer-to-ball direction is roughly 90° from the observer-to-lamp direction. Keep the lamp fixed.
  4. Look at the ball, not the lamp. Sketch the visible bright fraction; compare it with the prediction. Rotate the ball around the observer to compare new and full phases. Use the paper diagram instead if no lamp is available.

Conceptual positions only — not to scale. A nearby lamp produces only an approximate quarter-phase demonstration. Do not use the real Sun or a hot lamp.

Student-friendly CER prompts

Claim
“At First Quarter, how much of the Moon’s visible circle is bright?”
Evidence
“What did you see on the ball, in your drawing, or in the Moon model? Record two specific observations.”
Reasoning
“How do the lamp, observer and ball positions explain the evidence? Why is it not Earth’s shadow?”
Show a sample student response (hypothetical, not collected from pupils)

Claim: At First Quarter, about half the Moon’s visible circle looks bright. Evidence: When I held the ball to the side of the lamp, I saw a bright half and a dark half; moving the ball changed which part I could see. Reasoning: The lamp lit one side of the ball all the time, but my viewing position changed how much of that lit side I saw. Earth’s shadow was not needed.

Differentiate: For emerging writers, offer sentence stems and allow labelled sketches instead of full paragraphs. For students ready for extension, compare Last Quarter and discuss why the real Moon’s orientation differs by location; ask what this lamp model leaves out (real sizes, distances and orbital tilt).

Exit check: “Why can the Moon be half bright at First Quarter when its name says quarter?” Look for the distinction between fraction of the cycle and visible illuminated disk.

What's simplified

This model compresses a few things to stay readable.

Every visualization on Space Atlas states what has been simplified, so a model is never mistaken for the evidence itself.

01

Distance

The real Earth–Moon distance is about 30 Earth diameters. Here it's compressed so the Moon stays visible next to Earth.

02

Tilt

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° from Earth's orbit around the Sun. This model flattens that tilt, which is also why real eclipses are rarer than this diagram would suggest.

03

Size

Earth and the Moon are enlarged relative to the orbit so both stay clearly visible and clickable-scale.
