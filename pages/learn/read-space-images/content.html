Is that space photo actually a photo?

A photograph, an artist's concept, a simulation, and a scientific diagram can all be useful — but they do not make the same claim. Tap each card below for a real example.

Visual model

Three kinds of truth a space image can show.

OBSERVATION

Recorded by an instrument

Ask which telescope, spacecraft, wavelength, date, and processing steps produced the image or measurement.

Tap for an example ▾
Example: Hubble's photo of Jupiter combines several exposures taken through separate color filters, then aligned and color-composited — it is real data, but the final color image is assembled, not a single snapshot.
MODEL / SIMULATION

A calculation made visible

Ask which variables were simplified, whether time or distance is compressed, and which data constrain the model.

Tap for an example ▾
Example: every 3D scene on Space Atlas is a model — planet sizes and Moon distances are exaggerated so small worlds stay visible and clickable. The motion is real, but sped up and compressed.
ILLUSTRATION

An explanation, not a photograph

Useful for things we cannot photograph directly. A trustworthy source should label an artist's concept or schematic clearly.

Tap for an example ▾
Example: images of a black hole's accretion disk or an exoplanet's surface are usually artist's concepts built from indirect data — no camera has ever photographed either directly in visible light.
What's simplified

Even real observations go through a lot of processing.

01

False color

Many NASA images use "false color" — mapping infrared, X-ray, or radio data onto colors human eyes can see. The colors are not what your eye would see.

02

Compositing

A single "photo" is often several exposures through different filters, stacked and aligned in software before publication.

03

Scale and crop

Captions rarely restate it, but nearly every published image is cropped, rotated, or rescaled for readability.
