Is that space photo actually a photo?

An observation, a simulation, and an artist's concept can all help explain space — but they provide different kinds of evidence. Examine the categories, then compare real Hubble and Webb observations of the same region.

Visual model

Three kinds of truth a space image can show.

OBSERVATION

Recorded by an instrument

Ask which telescope, spacecraft, wavelength, date, and processing steps produced the image or measurement.

Open example ↓
Example: Hubble's photo of Jupiter combines several exposures taken through separate color filters, then aligned and color-composited — it is real data, but the final color image is assembled, not a single snapshot.
MODEL / SIMULATION

A calculation made visible

Ask which variables were simplified, whether time or distance is compressed, and which data constrain the model.

Open example ↓
Example: Space Atlas 3D scenes are explanatory models, not telescope measurements. Planet sizes, distances and motion may be adjusted to make relationships visible; consult each model's scale note.
ILLUSTRATION

An explanation, not a photograph

Useful for things we cannot photograph directly. A trustworthy source should label an artist's concept or schematic clearly.

Open example ↓
Example: a detailed landscape view of a distant exoplanet is an artist's concept, not a resolved surface photograph. Black-hole environments can be observed, including at radio wavelengths, but dramatic side-on cutaway scenes are often scientific visualizations.

What changes when we observe different wavelengths?

On the left, Hubble emphasizes dusty pillars. On the right, Webb's near-infrared data reveals more stars and details within the dust. The difference is about wavelength and instruments — not a before-and-after change over eight years.

The telescope detectors record light through filters. Image processors assign visible display colors to those data layers, register them, and combine them. A color assignment is not evidence that the object emits that visible color to human eyes. The views also differ in resolution, exposure and image processing.

What's simplified

Even real observations go through a lot of processing.

01

False color

Many NASA images use "false color" — mapping infrared, X-ray, or radio data onto colors human eyes can see. The colors are not what your eye would see.

02

Compositing

A single "photo" is often several exposures through different filters, stacked and aligned in software before publication.

03

Scale and crop

Published images may be cropped, rotated, enhanced, or rescaled to communicate a specific result. Check an original release's full image and caption before interpreting what lies outside the frame.
