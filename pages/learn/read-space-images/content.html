The telescope detectors record light through filters. Image processors assign visible display colors to those data layers, register them, and combine them. How to tell what an image actually shows

First identify the source. Then decide what the image can and cannot establish.

01 · OBSERVATIONMeasured light, processed into a view

The Pillars images above come from real telescope detectors. Their color mappings are assigned, not what a person would see through an eyepiece.

Verify instrument · wavelengths · acquisition date · composite processing

02 · SIMULATIONA prediction from stated assumptions

Orbit animations calculate a simplified model. An appealing 3D representation does not mean planet sizes, distances, or speeds are shown to scale.

Verify input variables · model limits · scale · independent observations

03 · ARTIST'S CONCEPTAn interpretation, not a resolved photo

A detailed landscape on a distant exoplanet is not a photograph of its surface. Such views may be informed by scientific measurements, but depicted terrain is not observed detail.

Verify explicit concept label · observed facts · speculative features

An observation can be processed, a simulation can use observations, and an illustration can be scientifically informed. These labels describe how evidence supports the image, not a ranking of visual quality. Read the original caption and source before drawing conclusions.

What's simplified

Even real observations go through a lot of processing.

01

False color

Many NASA images use "false color" — mapping infrared, X-ray, or radio data onto colors human eyes can see. The colors are not what your eye would see.

02

Compositing

A single "photo" is often several exposures through different filters, stacked and aligned in software before publication.

03

Scale and crop

Published images may be cropped, rotated, enhanced, or rescaled to communicate a specific result. Check an original release's full image and caption before interpreting what lies outside the frame.
