A. Moon Phases · First Quarter

Open the Moon Phases lesson

  1. Ask: “At First Quarter, how much of the Moon we see is bright: 25%, 50%, or 100%?”
  2. Ask for the prediction before moving the Moon.
  3. Have the learner choose, compare, and explain whether their prediction matched.

Scientific evidence for educator: approximately 50% of the visible Moon's disk is illuminated. “Quarter” means one-quarter of the lunar phase cycle, not one-quarter of the disk.

B. Moon Eclipses · When nodes miss

Open the Moon Eclipses lesson

  1. Ask: “Does every New Moon produce a solar eclipse?”
  2. With orbital tilt enabled, inspect node direction 90° away from Sun–Earth line.
  3. Move nodes to 0° along that line; compare the side-on Moon's vertical offset and readout. Try the flat, hypothetical orbit only as an explicit comparison.

Scientific evidence for educator: New Moon is necessary for solar eclipse geometry but not sufficient. The inclined Moon orbit must also pass near a node. The 15° visual tilt is exaggerated from the real approximately 5.1°.

C. Transit method · Blocked starlight

Open the Transit Method lesson

  1. Ask: “What changes in star brightness when a planet passes in front?”
  2. At phase 90°, use planet radius 10% then 20% of the star's radius.
  3. Set phase 0° and compare the light curve. Ask for an explanation of the observed result.

Scientific evidence for educator: the simplified uniform-brightness model gives 99%, then 96% star brightness at central transit, then 100% outside transit. Changing radius changes blocked area quadratically.

D. Galaxy viewing angle · Projection, not a new object

Open the Galaxy Types lesson

  1. Ask: “Does a spiral galaxy become a different type when seen edge-on?”
  2. Move the projection angle from 0° to 80°. Ask what actually changed.
  3. Optionally open its 3D model and compare to the labeled 2D view.

Scientific evidence for educator: the observation angle changes; the galaxy's physical type does not. The 3D particles are generated examples, not recorded stellar coordinates.
